# Typing the Map: Why TipeAndo Is a Typing Game About Roads

*What research says about typing fluency, memory and learning through play, and how a small Argentine typing game tries to build on it.*

> **Read the interactive version (in Spanish):** [tipeando.com.ar/ensayo.html](https://tipeando.com.ar/ensayo.html). It has a typing test, a dual-task demo and a mini route you can play right in the article.

---

TipeAndo didn't start with a teaching question. It started with something simpler: a map of Argentina with its National Routes drawn on it, and the idea of traveling them by typing. You pick a route and type the names of the towns along it, in order, while a marker moves down the road.

But once the game started working, an uncomfortable question showed up: is it good for anything besides passing the time?

This essay tries to answer that honestly. It is not a study of TipeAndo. The game collects no data, so we can't claim it improves anything. What we can do is show what research says about the three things the game combines (typing fluently, remembering what you write and learning through play) and explain how each design decision tries to lean on that evidence.

---

## 1. Your hands steal attention from your head

Writing means doing two things at once: deciding what to say and carrying it out. While the carrying out isn't automatic yet, it competes for the same attention the content needs.

One study with university students measured this directly. Those with weaker typing skills showed more cognitive effort while typing and produced lower-quality texts ([Alves, Castro & Olive, 2008](https://doi.org/10.1080/00207590701398951)). With 276 students in grades 8 and 9, the link between how fluently students produced letters and the quality of their writing was stronger for typed texts than for handwritten ones ([Christensen, 2004](https://doi.org/10.1348/0007099042376373)).

*[GIF: the dual-task demo. A reader types a short sentence normally, then again while counting backwards from 100 by threes, and sees both speeds side by side.]*

In the interactive version you can try this yourself: type a sentence, then type it again while counting backwards from 100 by threes. It's a demonstration, not an experiment. But if the second round felt harder, you got a small taste of what happens to someone for whom every key is a search: the head is busy with something else.

The good news is that this can be trained. In the same work by Christensen, a typing practice program improved the writing quality of slow typists more than writing a daily journal on a computer. And a meta-analysis on teaching writing to adolescents found that writing with a word processor improves text quality, with an effect size of 0.55 ([Graham & Perin, 2007](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.99.3.445)).

**What TipeAndo does with this:** the game is, first of all, typing practice. The words are short and familiar (town names), so the effort goes into the typing itself rather than into thinking about what to write.

---

## 2. How fast do you really type?

The largest reference we have comes from 168,000 people who took an online typing test in English. They averaged about 52 words per minute, with around 1.2% uncorrected errors, and the fastest exceeded 120 WPM ([Dhakal, Feit, Kristensson & Oulasvirta, 2018](https://doi.org/10.1145/3173574.3174220)).

*[Image: the 30-second speed test result, showing a reader's WPM on a bar with marks at 52 (average) and 120 (fastest).]*

The interesting part of that study isn't the average. It's what separates fast typists from everyone else: how they chain keystrokes, alternating hands and fingers. And you don't need classic ten-finger touch typing. Self-taught typists using about six fingers matched the speed and accuracy of people using the "proper" technique ([Feit, Weir & Oulasvirta, 2016](https://doi.org/10.1145/2858036.2858233)). What matters is sustained practice, not the method.

**What TipeAndo does with this:** it doesn't teach a technique or correct finger placement. It measures WPM, accuracy and combos, and keeps your best score for each route, so you compete against yourself.

---

## 3. What practice buys you

Typing faster isn't a medal. It's time, and time can be calculated:

> **hours saved per year = words per day × days per year × (1 / current WPM − 1 / target WPM) ÷ 60**

Going from 15 to 35 WPM, typing 1,500 words a day, 200 days a year, comes out to roughly 190 hours a year. It's arithmetic, not a measured result, but it helps size up why it's worth practicing something we use every day. (The interactive version lets you plug in your own numbers.)

---

## 4. Learning the map by typing it

This is TipeAndo's most particular bet: using typing not only as a skill but as a way to memorize.

Two effects from memory research support it. The first is the **production effect**: producing a word leaves a stronger trace than reading it silently, and this has been observed when saying it aloud, writing it by hand and also typing it ([Forrin, MacLeod & Ozubko, 2012](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13421-012-0210-8)). The second is **retrieval practice**: pulling something out of memory strengthens it more than studying it again. In the classic experiment, people who practiced by recalling retained more after two days and after a week than those who reread the material ([Roediger & Karpicke, 2006](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2006.01693.x)).

*[GIF: the mini route. A reader types four or five town names in order and a marker travels along the road; then the names disappear and the reader has to recall the order.]*

**What TipeAndo does with this:** each route is traveled by typing its towns in order while you see where they are. Replaying a route means retrieving those names and their places again. This ties into Argentina's national curriculum for social studies (the *Núcleos de Aprendizajes Prioritarios*), which includes the country's political division and how it's represented on maps ([NAP Ciencias Sociales, Res. CFCyE 235/05](https://cfe.educacion.gob.ar/resoluciones/res05/235-05-ane1-cs.pdf)).

An honest caveat: the game currently shows the names while you type them, so it's closer to production than to retrieval. Retrieval shows up when you replay routes and already know which town comes next.

---

## 5. Why a game and not a drill

You could practice everything above with a list of towns and a stopwatch. So why a game?

Two meta-analyses found that educational digital games improve learning compared to instruction without games. The effect is moderate: 0.29 for learning and 0.36 for retention according to [Wouters et al. (2013)](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0031311), and 0.33 according to [Clark, Tanner-Smith & Killingsworth (2016)](https://doi.org/10.3102/0034654315582065). And it grows when the game is combined with instruction rather than replacing it.

*[Image: bar chart of the three effect sizes. For reference, 0.2 is considered small and 0.5 medium.]*

Each of TipeAndo's mechanics tries to push in the right direction:

- **Clean-town combo.** The combo grows with each town typed without mistakes and breaks with one. It rewards accuracy before speed, which is the right order when practicing.
- **Immediate feedback.** Every key is marked right or wrong instantly, with sound. You don't wait until the end to know what went wrong.
- **Short, repeatable routes.** A run takes minutes, restarts with a single key and can be replayed to beat your own score.
- **Unlocks.** Keyboard sound packs unlock as you play: a small reward that invites you back.

None of these mechanics has been evaluated on its own. They are design decisions informed by evidence, not results.

---

## 6. What we don't know yet

It's worth saying clearly:

- **There is no data on TipeAndo.** The game has no server and no sign-up; progress stays in each browser. Everything above justifies the design, but it doesn't prove it works.
- **The evidence comes from similar contexts, not identical ones.** The speed studies are in English; the memory studies use word lists, not maps; the game meta-analyses average very different games.
- **The production and retrieval effects are measured over days or weeks**, not months. We don't know how much of a route sticks after a year.

What we can do is measure. A school that wants to use TipeAndo could record WPM and accuracy at the start and end of four weeks, and ask students to place towns on a blank map before and after. If you'd like to try it in a classroom, we'd love to hear how it goes.

---

## Closing

TipeAndo is a small bet: that practicing something useful (typing) and learning something of your own (your country's map) can be the same activity, and that this activity can be fun. Research doesn't guarantee it works, but it does say each of those pieces makes sense.

The best way to find out is to play it: [tipeando.com.ar](https://tipeando.com.ar). The game is in Spanish.
